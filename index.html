Finding buried walls from the air

Buried walls change the ground above them. They hold heat differently from the surrounding soil, alter how plants grow, and leave faint rises in the terrain. My MSc research built a workflow that measures all three with drones, learns their signature at a site whose architecture is already documented, and applies it to a site that has never been systematically surveyed.

  1. Survey the ground four ways

    Colour, thermal, multispectral and LiDAR flights were made the morning after rainfall, when damp soil exaggerates the thermal contrast over buried stone. Each sensor becomes a set of layers describing the same patch of ground.

    Choose a layer to compare what each sensor sees at Thermi. Arrow keys also work.

  2. Anchor the data in the excavated record

    The Ephorate’s excavation plan was georeferenced onto the new orthomosaic and a 1.5 m grid was laid over the site. Each cell was labelled as over known architecture or not, after masking exposed masonry, deep shadow and dense vegetation that would mislead the sensors.

  3. Model where buried walls are likely

    Sensor layers were condensed by principal component analysis, filtered for significance and redundancy, and fed to a logistic regression. The model is deliberately simple: each component’s contribution can be read from its coefficient, so a prediction can be traced back to its physical evidence.

  4. Turn probabilities into places to dig

    A probability map does not tell a field team where to start. A two-stage cluster analysis, Getis-Ord Gi* followed by Local Moran’s I, keeps only statistically stable concentrations of high probability.

    Transferred to Arisvi without refitting, the model narrowed the site to 1,440 priority cells covering 3,240 m², a thirteen-fold reduction in the area to investigate.

Current work: explanations archaeologists can check

Deep-learning models already flag candidate features in aerial data, but rarely say why, and natural landforms are routinely mistaken for walls. My doctoral research develops a multimodal model that fuses LiDAR, thermal and multispectral data, paired with explainability methods (SHAP, Grad-CAM++) that attribute each detection to its physical evidence.

The central question is whether those explanations can be trusted. The project will test their faithfulness directly and run a study in which archaeologists interpret the same data with and without them. Fieldwork takes place within the Western Megaris Archaeological Landscape Project (WeMALP), with UAV seasons planned from September 2026. Data and code will be released openly, with sensitive site locations withheld in line with Ephorate guidance.

Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Christos Vasilakos, Remote Sensing & GIS Research Group, University of the Aegean.
